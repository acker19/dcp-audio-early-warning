ASSET HEALTH INDEX (AHI) – ADDITIONAL FAQ

Q: What is the Asset Health Index (AHI)?
A: The Asset Health Index (AHI) is a numerical score between 0 and 1 that represents the current health condition of a machine based on its vibration characteristics.

Q: Why is AHI important?
A: AHI provides a simple way to understand machine health without requiring users to interpret complex vibration data.

Q: What does an AHI of 1.0 mean?
A: An AHI of 1.0 indicates that the machine is operating under normal conditions with no significant abnormalities detected.

Q: What does an AHI of 0.85 mean?
A: An AHI of 0.85 indicates the machine is healthy and operating within acceptable limits.

Q: What does an AHI of 0.75 mean?
A: An AHI of 0.75 indicates a warning condition. Early signs of degradation have been detected, and the machine should be monitored more closely.

Q: What does an AHI of 0.50 mean?
A: An AHI of 0.50 indicates that the machine has noticeable degradation and maintenance should be scheduled soon.

Q: What does an AHI of 0.20 mean?
A: An AHI of 0.20 indicates the machine is in a critical condition and immediate inspection is recommended.

Q: What happens if the AHI reaches zero?
A: An AHI close to zero indicates extremely poor machine health and a very high risk of failure.

Q: Why does AHI decrease?
A: AHI decreases when abnormal vibration patterns become more severe.

Q: Can AHI increase again?
A: Yes. After maintenance, repairs, or replacement of faulty components, the AHI may improve if the machine returns to normal operating conditions.

Q: Is a low AHI always caused by bearing faults?
A: No. A low AHI indicates abnormal machine behavior, which may result from bearing faults, imbalance, misalignment, looseness, or other mechanical issues.

Q: What data is used to calculate AHI?
A: The system uses RMS, kurtosis, anomaly score, and energy deviation to estimate machine health.

Q: What is RMS?
A: RMS measures the overall vibration energy of the machine.

Q: Why is RMS important?
A: Increasing RMS values often indicate increasing vibration severity and possible machine degradation.

Q: What is kurtosis?
A: Kurtosis measures the presence of impulsive vibration events that commonly occur with damaged bearings or repeated impacts.

Q: Why is kurtosis included in the calculation?
A: Kurtosis helps detect faults that may not significantly increase overall vibration levels but produce sharp impact events.

Q: What is the anomaly score?
A: The anomaly score estimates how different the current machine behavior is compared to healthy operating conditions.

Q: How is the anomaly score generated?
A: It is computed from vibration features using the system's anomaly detection algorithm.

Q: What is energy deviation?
A: Energy deviation measures how vibration energy changes under different operating loads.

Q: Why is energy deviation included?
A: Machines often operate under different loads, and energy deviation helps account for these variations.

Q: What is the purpose of load factors?
A: Load factors adjust the AHI calculation to reflect different operating conditions.

Q: Why is smoothing applied?
A: Smoothing reduces temporary fluctuations and prevents unnecessary alerts caused by short-duration vibration spikes.

Q: What smoothing method is used?
A: A rolling average over 30 consecutive snapshots.

Q: Why is a rolling average used?
A: It produces more stable health estimates and reduces false alarms.

Q: Why isn't AHI updated from a single vibration reading?
A: Single readings may contain noise or transient events that do not accurately represent the machine's condition.

Q: How often is AHI updated?
A: AHI is recalculated whenever new vibration data is processed.

Q: What is the alert threshold?
A: The system generates alerts when the calculated AHI falls below the configured warning threshold.

Q: What is the critical threshold?
A: Machines with an AHI of 0.20 or lower are classified as Critical.

Q: What do the AHI status levels mean?

Healthy:
AHI between 0.80 and 1.00.

Warning:
AHI between 0.68 and 0.80.

Degraded:
AHI between 0.20 and 0.68.

Critical:
AHI less than or equal to 0.20.

Q: Can users change AHI thresholds?
A: Depending on system configuration, administrators may adjust alert thresholds to suit specific applications.

Q: Does AHI predict the exact failure time?
A: No. AHI estimates the current health condition and likelihood of degradation but does not provide an exact failure date.

Q: Can AHI replace physical inspections?
A: No. AHI is a decision-support tool that complements regular maintenance and inspections.

Q: What happens if AHI suddenly drops?
A: A sudden drop may indicate rapid fault development, sensor issues, or significant changes in operating conditions.

Q: Why is my AHI fluctuating?
A: Small fluctuations are normal and may result from changes in machine load or operating conditions.

Q: What should I do if AHI remains in the Warning range?
A: Increase monitoring frequency and schedule a maintenance inspection.

Q: What should I do if AHI remains in the Degraded range?
A: Plan maintenance as soon as practical and monitor the machine closely.

Q: What should I do if AHI reaches the Critical range?
A: Stop the machine if safe to do so, inspect it immediately, and repair or replace faulty components.

Q: Is AHI the only indicator I should monitor?
A: No. Users should also review RMS, kurtosis, anomaly score, trends, and alert history for a complete assessment.

Q: Can AHI be used for different machine types?
A: Yes. The AHI framework can be adapted for various rotating machines, although thresholds and models may need adjustment.

Q: Why is trend analysis more important than a single AHI value?
A: A trend shows whether machine health is improving, stable, or deteriorating over time, enabling better maintenance decisions.

Q: How does the dashboard display AHI?
A: AHI is shown as a numerical value, a color-coded health status, and historical trend graphs.

Q: What datasets were used to develop and validate the AHI approach?
A: The system was developed and evaluated using publicly available datasets such as MIMII and PRONOSTIA, which contain vibration data from healthy and faulty machines.

Q: Can future versions improve AHI?
A: Yes. Future enhancements may include adaptive thresholds, machine-specific models, additional sensor inputs, and Remaining Useful Life (RUL) estimation.
